## 1. Executive summary
### Why this report
The Cloud and AI Development Act (CADA) was proposed by the European Commission on 3 June 2026 as the centrepiece of the Tech Sovereignty Package. It is the first Union law to turn digital sovereignty into verifiable requirements for the public procurement of cloud and AI services. It pursues two objectives: to triple Europe's data centre capacity within five to seven years, and to bring order to the public market through four sovereignty assurance levels and a mandatory risk assessment in the public sector [R1][R2].
This report analyses the proposal from the place where it is decided how technology reaches businesses and public administrations in Spain and Portugal: the distribution channel. It follows the entire value chain. At one end are the vendors of data infrastructure, servers, storage, networking, cybersecurity, and components for data centres and AI. In the middle are the value-added distributors that represent them on the Iberian Peninsula and the hundreds of resellers and integrators that buy from them to design, deploy and operate solutions. At the other end are the industrial and service companies that use that technology.
The question guiding this document is a practical one: what the CADA changes in the business of each link in the chain, what bottlenecks it may cause and what opportunities it opens up for those who prepare early.
### Key conclusions
- The CADA does not regulate the entire cloud market, but access to the public market. However, the assurance level will become a commercial seal that private customers in critical sectors (energy, health, finance, water, transport) will also demand [R1][R3].
- The assurance level is granted to each service, not to each company. That is why sovereignty is built along the chain: the integrator providing the service needs evidence that only the hardware and software vendor can supply and, increasingly, the distributor that supplies, configures and supports them.
- For vendors, the CADA introduces a new competitive dimension: where data and telemetry are processed, where support is delivered from, who controls the company and what its software supply chain contains. Those who document these answers in advance will be preferred by the channel.
- For integrators, level 1 is achievable through self-assessment. Levels 2 to 4 require audits, certification and architectures under European control, which few will be able to build on their own.
- For value-added distributors, the regulation strengthens their role as orchestrators: the multi-vendor catalogue, technical pre-sales, logistics within the Union, financing and training are exactly the resources an ecosystem that must prove sovereignty needs.
- The most likely bottlenecks are certification (the European EUCS scheme remains blocked), the shortage of European capacity and GPUs, memory price pressure in 2026, the talent gap and the risk of each Member State applying the regulation in its own way.
### Expected timeline
The proposal is going through the legislative process. The European Parliament presented its joint draft report on 11 November 2026, will debate it in committee on 1 December and expects to vote on it in committee around March 2027 and in plenary in April [R4][R5]. The political goal is an agreement in the fourth quarter of 2027. In the baseline scenario, the first practical obligations would arrive between 2028 and 2029, and the first tenders requiring specific levels between 2029 and 2030.
### What it means for the value chain
The Iberian channel comes to the CADA in good shape. The Spanish distribution market grew by 15.1 % in 2025 according to CONTEXT [R6], and one of the largest distributors on the peninsula, the Esprinet Group, increased its revenue by 21 % in Spain and 37 % in Portugal in the first half of 2026 [R7]. That strength is the basis for investing in what the CADA will require: documentary evidence, tiered architectures and service capabilities.
> Key message: sovereignty is not demonstrated by a single player in isolation. It is demonstrated by a documented supply chain. The value-added distributor that organises that chain for its vendors and integrators will enjoy an advantage that is hard to replicate between 2027 and 2030.

## 2. The Iberian technology channel and the role of the value-added distributor
### How technology reaches the end customer
In Europe, and especially in Spain and Portugal, most professional technology is not sold directly by the vendor. It travels along a two-tier chain: the vendor sells to the distributor, the distributor sells to the reseller or integrator, and the latter sells, deploys and operates the solution for the end customer. This model exists because no vendor can efficiently manage thousands of relationships with small and medium-sized resellers; the distributor aggregates demand, finances inventory, takes on credit risk and provides logistics and technical training [R8][R9].
Margins at each link are thin. In hardware, the distributor's margin is usually between 3 % and 8 %, offset by volume, vendor incentives and value-added services; the combined margins of distributor and reseller can account for between 35 % and 50 % of the final price depending on the category [R8]. That is why every regulatory change that adds work to the chain must be turned into a billable service or a commercial advantage if it is not to erode profitability.
### The weight of the channel in technology spending
According to Omdia, partners channelled 70.1 % of global IT spending in 2025 and will channel 66.7 % in 2026: they are losing share to direct sales by the large providers, but their absolute volume keeps growing and their influence on decisions is increasing [R10]. Among SMEs in Europe, the Middle East and Africa, 82 % of IT spending goes through partners [R11], and in cybersecurity the proportion reaches 91.7 % [R12]. In Spain, the channel structure is highly fragmented: according to Dell, 98 % of its partners are SMEs [R13]. Moreover, the customer no longer decides alone: according to Canalys, the B2B buyer goes through an average of 28 touchpoints before choosing a supplier, accompanied by several trusted partners, which gives the channel influence beyond the transaction itself [R14].
### Broadline distributors and value-added distributors
Two types of distributor coexist on the peninsula. Broadline distributors (TD SYNNEX, Ingram Micro, Esprinet, MCR, Infortisa, DMI, Globomatik, among others) move workplace, peripheral and consumer volume with logistical efficiency [R15]. Value-added distributors, such as Arrow, V-Valley, Exclusive Networks or Westcon-Comstor, specialise in data centre, networking, cybersecurity, cloud and AI, with technical pre-sales, labs, training, professional services and financing.
### The value-added distributor in figures
The large distribution groups work with hundreds of vendors and tens of thousands of resellers: the Esprinet Group, for example, reports more than 650 vendors and more than 31,000 resellers in southern Europe [R16], and in 2025 it posted gross revenue of EUR 1,765.4 million in Spain (+10 %) and EUR 106 million in Portugal (+47 %) [R17]. In the first half of 2026 its revenue grew by 21 % in Spain and 37 % in Portugal [R7]. In 2024, its value division contributed around 25 % of turnover and more than 60 % of the group's EBITDA [R18]: added value is the engine of the distributor's profitability.
Value-added distributors are broadening their catalogues with recent agreements in cybersecurity, observability, physical infrastructure, mobility, digital energy and AI-ready colocation [R19][R20], and are setting up dedicated units for AI training and support for their partners [R21].
### Why the channel is where the CADA will be played out
The CADA measures the sovereignty of each service on the basis of data that is spread across the chain: the vendor knows where its equipment sends telemetry and who maintains its code; the distributor knows where first-line support is delivered from and how licences are managed; the integrator knows where the service runs and who operates it. No single link has all the information. Whoever gathers it, standardises it and makes it available to the others will become indispensable.
> The channel is not a passive intermediary under the CADA: it is the only point in the chain that sees the vendor, the integrator and the customer at the same time.

## 3. What the CADA is: origin, architecture and timeline
### From Draghi to the Tech Sovereignty Package
The idea of a European cloud and AI development act first appeared in the Draghi report on competitiveness (September 2024). The Commission incorporated it into the AI Continent Action Plan (April 2025), opened a call for evidence in 2025 and adopted proposal COM(2026) 502 on 3 June 2026, together with the Chips Act 2.0, the European Open Source Strategy and a roadmap for the digitalisation of the energy sector [R1][R2][R22]. Its legal basis is Articles 114 and 173(3) TFEU, and it takes the form of a regulation, directly applicable in all Member States.
### The diagnosis
The Commission identifies two problems. The first is insufficient and concentrated computing capacity: AI demand is growing faster than Europe's data centre supply. The second is dependency: the share of European cloud providers fell from 29 % in 2017 to 15 % in 2022, while three non-European hyperscalers hold more than 70 % of the market [R1].
### Architecture of the proposal
The proposal is organised into five titles, 48 articles and three annexes [R1]:
- Title I: subject matter and definitions.
- Title II (Arts. 3–9): cloud and AI leadership initiatives, AI Centres built on the digital innovation hubs, national strategies and frontier AI projects.
- Title III (Arts. 10–15): data centre acceleration zones, single points of information, permits and strategic projects.
- Title IV (Arts. 16–44): sovereignty framework with four levels, recognition, auditing, authorities, public-sector risk assessment, procurement, EuroCloud Federation, joint procurement and open source.
- Title V (Arts. 45–48): delegated acts, review and entry into force.
- Annexes: major technological challenges (I), requirements for each level (II) and audit evidence (III).
### What the CADA is not
The CADA does not prohibit any provider from operating in the private market, does not impose data localisation on all companies and does not replace the AI Act. Its direct obligations fall on the public sector and on providers that seek recognition of a level. Its indirect effect, however, will be broad, because Article 31 allows voluntary assessments by critical private entities and empowers the Commission to make them mandatory in highly critical sectors [R1].
### Timeline
As of 14 November 2026, the CADA is at first reading. In the Council it is being examined in the telecommunications working party; the Irish Presidency is preparing a progress report for the end of the year and the Legal Service considers the proposal compatible with the WTO, with adjustments to the European preferences [R23]. In Parliament, the rapporteurs presented the joint draft report on 11 November 2026; the committee debate is scheduled for 1 December, the committee vote for mid-March 2027 and the plenary vote for April [R5]. The draft calls for more investment in European cloud and AI infrastructure, greater protection of critical sectors, more public procurement from European providers and faster permitting for data centres. One of the rapporteurs, Diego Solier, has indicated that the requirements could be softened and that the rules for international companies will be clarified [R4]. The text enters into force twenty days after its publication and applies one year later, with staggered deadlines for Member States (the European Economic and Social Committee and the European Data Protection Supervisor have already issued their opinions [R24][R25]): six months for acceleration zones, twelve for authorities, strategies and risk assessments, and a maximum of twelve months to migrate services where the assessment requires it.
### What it means for the channel
The timeline offers a two-year preparation window. For a vendor, it is time to document its supply chain and adapt support contracts. For a value-added distributor, it is time to classify its catalogue and design evidence services. For an integrator, it is time to inventory its services and decide which ones it will take to higher levels.
> Timeline recommendation: 2027 as the year of diagnosis and offer design, 2028 as the year of evidence and alliances, and 2029 as the year of the first tenders with CADA requirements.

## 4. Titles II and III: leadership, frontier AI and data centres
### The leadership initiatives
Articles 3 to 6 create a Cloud Leadership Initiative and an AI Leadership Initiative with R&D objectives: next-generation cloud and AI technologies, open and efficient stacks covering everything from the edge to data, and industrial, physical and frontier AI [R1][R3]. They have no budget of their own: they will be implemented through Horizon Europe, the Digital Europe Programme and the future European Competitiveness Fund. Article 5 creates a network of AI Centres built on the European Digital Innovation Hubs (EDIHs), which will bring AI experimentation closer to SMEs and public administrations, in line with the Commission's Apply AI strategy [R26].
### National strategies
Article 7 requires each Member State to adopt a national cloud and AI strategy within twelve months, aligned with the Digital Decade (75 % of companies using cloud, big data and AI, and 10,000 secure edge nodes) and including measures to support stacks based on open hardware and software [R1]. These strategies will translate into public procurement plans and SME adoption plans, which represent direct demand for the channel.
### Frontier AI and gigafactories
Articles 8 and 9 make it possible to recognise priority frontier AI projects and allocate computing to them in coordination with the AI Factories and the future EuroHPC AI gigafactories. The gigafactory call, which closed on 12 November 2026, provides for up to seven facilities with more than 100,000 AI processors each [R27]. Spain has submitted a joint Madrid–Catalonia bid.
### Acceleration zones and strategic projects
Title III addresses physical scarcity. Article 10 requires the designation of at least one data centre acceleration zone within six months; Article 13 caps at twelve months the permitting decision for projects of the highest relevance; Article 14 allows the designation of strategic projects that meet at least two of five criteria: essential public functions, sustainability or innovation, grid stability, use of chips, servers or quantum computers manufactured in the Union, or relief of an identified computing shortage [R1]. Data centres with European chips or a smaller energy footprint could obtain priority grid connection and better network tariffs [R22].
### What it means for vendors, distributors and integrators
For vendors of servers, storage, power and cooling, Title III is a demand-side policy: more data centres mean more orders for critical infrastructure. The criterion of hardware manufactured in the Union may reward those with European production or assembly, and the Annex I target (average PUE of 1.15 and server utilisation of around 50 %) favours more efficient equipment. For value-added distributors, which distribute data centre infrastructure and sign agreements with power and cooling vendors and colocation operators [R19][R20], the wave of investment is the most tangible short-term opportunity. For integrators, the AI Centres and national strategies open up adoption projects among SMEs and public administrations, and the AI Factories offer computing to fine-tune sector-specific models.
> Title III turns sovereignty into megawatts: the chain that supplies efficient servers, power, cooling and racks to the new European data centres will capture the first wave of value from the CADA.

## 5. The sovereignty framework: four assurance levels
### The core of the proposal
Article 16 creates the Union's cloud sovereignty framework with four assurance levels set out in Annex II. It transposes into binding law criteria on ownership, control and exposure to foreign jurisdictions that had been removed from the EUCS certification scheme for lack of agreement among Member States [R28]. Law firms that have analysed the proposal stress that, in practice, it also creates new compliance tiers for software providers [R29]. The levels are voluntary for providers, but they determine which services the administration may procure for each activity.
### What each level requires
Level 1. Provider established in the Union; infrastructure and assets, including those of subcontractors, in the Union unless the public customer requests otherwise; customer data, metadata and telemetry in the Union; support outsourced outside the EU that does not compromise operational autonomy; state-of-the-art cybersecurity; declared subcontractors; and, where there is third-country control, assurance that no foreign law requires vulnerabilities to be disclosed before they are known to be exploited [R1].
Level 2. In addition: provider, subcontractors, infrastructure and staff in the Union; European cybersecurity certificate at "substantial" level (or national schemes until one exists); prohibition on using service data to train AI of third-country entities; support initiated and delivered exclusively from the Union; software supply chain controls (SBOM, control of remote functions, code audits of third-country components, migration plans); and legal, technical and organisational separation from the non-EU parent company [R1].
Level 3. Staff with Union citizenship and, where applicable, security clearance; "substantial" certificate; provider not controlled by a third country, save for a derogation linked to a recognised third country with safeguards such as reasonable access to the code [R1].
Level 4. No third-country control; sensitive data always in the Union; "high" certificate; and effective control of the software supply chain, so that no third country controls the design, development, maintenance or evolution of the components [R1].
### How it is demonstrated
Level 1 is demonstrated by self-assessment, and SME declarations are valid throughout the Union without prior recognition (Arts. 17(3) and 19). Levels 2 to 4 require an independent audit with the evidence set out in Annex III (Art. 20); recognised services are entered in a central Commission repository and material changes must be notified (Arts. 22 and 23) [R1].
### Why moving between levels is a matter for the whole chain
The critical jump is between levels 1 and 2. Level 2 requires support to be delivered from the Union, the software supply chain to be documented and data not to train third-party models. None of these three conditions depends solely on the integrator: second- and third-line support is provided by the vendor; the SBOM is generated by the vendor; data use conditions are set in the vendor's contracts. And the distributor is usually the one managing first-line support, licences and spare-parts logistics. Levels 3 and 4 also depend on who controls the vendor, something neither the distributor nor the integrator can change.
### Example of a tiered catalogue
An integrator offering backup, virtualisation, cloud ERP and monitoring will assess each service separately. Backup on storage from a vendor with support in the Union, hosted in a European data centre, may aspire to level 2 or 3. ERP on a non-European hyperscaler may demonstrate level 1 if it meets the localisation, telemetry and transparency requirements. The right strategy is not to choose one level for the whole company, but to build a tiered catalogue with products the distributor has already classified.
### Case study: level 2 backup for a hospital
A regional integrator wants to offer a public hospital a managed backup service. If the risk assessment classifies electronic health records as a public-order activity, the service will have to reach at least level 2. The integrator will need: a data centre established in the Union with staff in the Union; a storage system and data protection software whose advanced support is delivered from the Union; a management console that does not send telemetry outside Europe; the SBOM of the components; and an accepted cybersecurity certification (in Spain, the National Security Framework (ENS) at high category until the European certificate exists). The integrator manufactures none of these pieces. If its distributor provides a reference configuration with vendors that already supply that evidence and a regional operator with an accredited level, the integrator can prepare its file in weeks rather than months.
> The level is granted to the service, but it is earned along the chain. A vendor that does not provide its share of the evidence leaves its integrators out of levels 2 to 4.

## 6. Evidence under Annexes II and III across the supply chain
### From principles to proof
Annex III defines how each requirement is demonstrated and turns sovereignty into a file that an auditor verifies [R1]. Its criteria are:
- A. Establishment in the Union: deeds, registered office, central administration and stable presence.
- B. Infrastructure, assets and staff: addresses of all infrastructure, including backups, disaster recovery and logs, and asset inventories.
- C. Data localisation: access logs, flow diagrams and contracts showing that there are no transfers outside the Union without approval.
- D. Staff citizenship: identities, organisation charts and access control.
- E. Cybersecurity certification: certificates under Regulation (EU) 2019/881 or, until a scheme exists, aligned with CEN/TS 18026:2024 or national schemes.
- F. Third-country AI: clauses prohibiting training on the data, MLOps logs in the EU, model cards and data lineage.
- G. Ownership and control: shareholders with at least 5 %, governance rules and a register of third-country access requests.
- H. Support from the Union: clauses, register of subcontractors, administrative access paths and access revocation.
- I. Software supply chain transparency.
### Who holds each piece of evidence
Read from the channel's perspective, Annex III is a division of labour. Criterion A and much of C are handled by the integrator or the service operator. B requires knowing where the equipment and spare parts are and who maintains them, information shared between distributor and integrator. E depends on the certification of the products and the service. Criteria F, G, H and I depend above all on the vendor: only the vendor can declare its ownership structure, where advanced support is delivered from, what remote functions its firmware or software includes and what third-country components it integrates.
### The role of the distributor
For a mid-sized integrator, requesting this information from dozens of vendors is not feasible. A value-added distributor can standardise it: ask each vendor for a sovereignty sheet per product family, keep it up to date, translate it into the language of Annex III and make it available to partners on its portal. It is a service similar to the one it already provides with datasheets, certifications, warranties and recycling or product safety compliance.
### Costs and difficulties
Criteria A, C and H are manageable for European providers. Criteria D, F, G and I are costly when the stack includes components from non-European vendors, because the evidence depends on information only they hold. The European Data Protection Supervisor has also asked that proof of staff citizenship respect data minimisation [R25].
### Tools
Practice will combine governance, risk and compliance platforms, automated asset inventories, SBOMs generated in continuous integration, data lineage logs for AI and standard clauses with suppliers. The verifiable credentials and self-descriptions of federated data spaces (Gaia-X, IDSA) can serve as a reusable format for exchanging evidence between companies [R30].
> A sovereignty sheet per product, maintained by the distributor with vendor data, can save each integrator weeks of work per service and become a de facto standard for the Iberian channel.
