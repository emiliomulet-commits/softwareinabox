## 20. Data centres and AI infrastructure on the Iberian Peninsula
### A wave of investment
According to the Spain DC association, Spain reached 439 MW of installed data centre capacity in 2025 and could attract EUR 66.9 billion of investment and more than 16,000 jobs by 2030 [R82]. Madrid, Barcelona and Aragon concentrate the large projects, with announcements such as AWS's in Aragon [R63]. Portugal attracts large-scale projects thanks to its Atlantic connectivity and renewable energy.
### What a data centre buys
A data centre is not just servers. Its purchasing chain includes power (uninterruptible power supplies, power distribution, backup generation), cooling (air, liquid, rear-door heat exchangers), racks and cabinets, structured cabling and networking, general-purpose and accelerated servers, storage, infrastructure management software, and physical and logical security. Value-added distributors have been working this chain for years: at the Newsbook round table on distributors in the data centre, value-added distributors stressed the need for a multi-vendor offering and financing to cover complete projects [R83].
### Distributor agreements in this field
In 2025 and 2026, value-added distributors strengthened their data centre offering with agreements with rack and cooling vendors, with power and cooling providers for data centres and with AI-ready colocation operators in regional data centres [R19][R20].
### The CADA and regional data centres
The CADA and the Spanish draft royal decree on data centres indirectly favour operators established in the Union, efficient and using renewables. Mid-sized regional and colocation data centres are the natural partners of mid-sized integrators, because they offer proximity, control and local support. But they are also the most exposed to energy requirements that demand high investment.
### AI at the edge and on the customer's premises
Not all AI will go to gigafactories. A growing share of inference will run at the edge (industrial plants, hospitals, logistics centres) or on the customer's premises, for reasons of latency, cost and sovereignty. This is mid-sized infrastructure, sold and installed by the channel.
### What it means for the value chain
For vendors of physical and IT infrastructure, the peninsula is one of the most dynamic markets in Europe; the CADA adds an argument based on efficiency and European hardware. For integrators, regional data centres with an accredited assurance level will be their platform for offering level 2 and 3 services. For the value-added distributor, the combination of a catalogue of physical infrastructure, servers, storage, colocation and financing enables it to offer "turnkey data centres" to regional operators and integrators.
> The data centre wave is the CADA's first tangible opportunity for the channel: every new megawatt translates into power, cooling, racks, servers and services.

## 21. Precedents: Commission procurement, SecNumCloud and Deutschland-Stack
### The Commission's Cloud Sovereignty Framework
In October 2025, the Commission launched a sovereign cloud tender for the European institutions worth up to EUR 180 million over six years, evaluated using its Cloud Sovereignty Framework, which measures sovereignty against eight objectives and assigns SEAL levels from 0 to 4, with SEAL-2 as the minimum [R84]. In April 2026 it awarded four contracts: Post Telecom with OVHcloud and Clever Cloud, STACKIT and Scaleway (SEAL-3), and Proximus with S3NS, Clarence and Mistral (SEAL-2). No provider reached SEAL-4, which requires a European chain from chips to software [R84]. CISPE criticised the fact that an offering based on hyperscaler technology could qualify as sovereign [R85].
### SecNumCloud in France
France requires its State Administration to host sensitive data in SecNumCloud-qualified services, ANSSI's framework with requirements for immunity from extraterritorial laws and control of capital; the 2024 SREN Law raised it to statutory status [R86]. Levels 3 and 4 of the CADA reproduce this logic.
### Deutschland-Stack in Germany
In March 2026 Germany adopted the Deutschland-Stack framework, with the open-source Sovereign Cloud Stack as its infrastructure standard, and awarded around EUR 250 million for a federal AI cloud with no US hyperscalers in the winning consortia. The Delos cloud, with Microsoft technology operated by a German company, remains the option for productivity [R87].
### Lessons for the channel
- Sovereignty can be measured and scored: buyers will do so.
- The highest level is almost unattainable in the short term: no provider reached SEAL-4, and CADA level 4 will have very few services.
- Hybrid models will play a role: non-European technology operated by European companies can reach intermediate levels.
- Open source is Germany's route to the higher levels.
- In every case consortia won, not individual companies: operator plus technology plus services.
### What it means for the value chain
The Commission's contract winners are consortia combining operator, technology and services, exactly the kind of alliance a value-added distributor can facilitate. For non-European vendors, the Delos, Bleu and S3NS models show that there is room at intermediate levels if they accept European operation and separation of control. For European vendors, the CADA's added-value criterion and the preference shown in these precedents are an advantage. For integrators, the lesson is that competition will take place in consortia.
> What the Commission is doing today with its own procurement anticipates what public administrations will do under the CADA: score sovereignty, require a minimum and award contracts to consortia.
| Precedent | Instrument | Outcome | Lesson for the channel |
| European Commission | Cloud Sovereignty Framework (SEAL 0–4) | 4 winners; none at SEAL-4 | Consortia and measurement |
| France | SecNumCloud and SREN Law | Obligation for sensitive data | Origin of levels 3–4 |
| Germany | Deutschland-Stack, federal AI cloud | EUR 250m with no US hyperscalers | Open stacks |

## 22. The debate and scenarios for the size of the sovereign segment
### Those calling for more sovereignty
CISPE considers that the CADA gets the sovereignty architecture right but falls short on procurement, because it does not require European alternatives to be considered, and warns that some clauses allow subcontractors to circumvent requirements [R85]. Free software associations regret that "open source first" has been watered down [R32], and advocates of a "EuroStack" call for an industrial policy covering the entire European technology stack [R88].
### Those warning of the costs
The US technology industry and part of the European one warn of the effect on competitiveness. An ECIPE study commissioned by CCIA Europe estimates a cost of around EUR 115 billion over ten years in its central scenario, with a range of EUR 35 billion to EUR 233 billion [R89]. Bruegel and ICLE point to the risk of making computing more expensive [R70][R90]. These figures should be read with caution: the study is funded by an interested party, and other analyses estimate GDP gains of up to EUR 480 billion in 2030 by combining sovereignty and AI adoption [R89].
### Where the negotiation is heading
Early signals point to a middle ground: softening requirements, clarifying rules for international companies, reducing the Commission's discretion and giving buyers more flexibility [R4]. The four levels and the risk assessment are likely to be retained, the derogations broadened and the recognition of third countries strengthened.
### Three scenarios for the size of the segment
- Soft: level 1 covers most public spending; levels 2 to 4 are a niche in defence, justice, security and health. The main effect is documentary.
- Baseline: level 1 is the general standard and levels 2 and 3 cover health, justice, taxation, security, critical infrastructure and part of the private NIS2 entities on a voluntary basis.
- Strict: broad public categories require levels 2 to 3, the Commission imposes assessments in highly critical private sectors and providers with no European alternative lose public market share.
### What it means for the value chain
The debate defines the size of the high-level market, not the existence of the regulation. The channel's strategy must work in all three scenarios: secure level 1 for the entire catalogue and prepare, with selected vendors, at least one level 2 and one level 3 reference offer in the highest-demand categories (storage and data protection, virtualisation and private cloud, security, local AI).
> The final CADA will probably be less strict in the details, but it will keep its architecture. Designing the strategy around the architecture reduces risk for the whole channel.

| Free software associations | More open source | A genuine "open source first" |

## 23. Impact on vendors selling through the channel
### A typology of vendors
A value-added distributor's catalogue brings together very different vendors, and the CADA affects them in different ways:
- Infrastructure hardware vendors (servers, storage, networking) headquartered outside the Union: their product is neutral in itself, but telemetry, cloud-based remote management, support and firmware fall within the evidence for levels 2 to 4.
- Physical infrastructure vendors (power, cooling, racks): low sovereignty impact, high demand and efficiency impact.
- Non-European software and security vendors with cloud services: they are directly affected by data localisation, support from the Union, use of data to train AI and control.
- European software and security vendors: potential advantage from the European added-value criterion and from levels 3 and 4.
- Open-source solutions with commercial support: advantage at higher levels if support is delivered from the Union.
### What integrators will ask of each vendor
- A clear statement of where telemetry, logs and diagnostic data are processed and stored, and whether they can be kept in the Union or disabled.
- Local management options or management from a European region, with no mandatory dependence on cloud consoles outside the Union.
- Second- and third-line support delivered from the Union, with controlled and logged remote access.
- Product SBOMs and a vulnerability management policy compliant with the CRA.
- Clauses guaranteeing that customer data is not used to train AI models.
- Information on ownership structure and on access requests from third-country authorities.
### What risks they face
The main risk for non-European vendors is being excluded from levels 3 and 4 in the most sensitive public activities, and from levels 2 to 4 if they do not offer European support and telemetry. A second risk is designation as a high-risk supplier under the CSA2 [R40]. A third is reputational: if a competitor publishes its sovereignty sheet first, the channel will tend to recommend it.
### What opportunities they have
The vendor that adapts its product (disconnected operating modes, European management regions, support in the Union, software supply chain documentation) can sell more in a market that is fragmenting. Workload repatriation from the public cloud to private or regional environments increases demand for infrastructure. And the data centre wave multiplies demand for equipment.
### How the distributor can help them
The value-added distributor can offer its vendors an Iberian channel readiness service: a sovereignty sheet template per product family, review with its technical team, publication on the partner portal, integrator training, reference configurations per level and presence in public framework agreements. For a vendor without a local structure, this reduces the cost of entering the sovereign segment in Spain and Portugal.
### A sovereignty sheet in practice
The sheet we propose for each product family takes up one or two pages and answers ten questions: the entity marketing the product in the Union; control structure; location of telemetry, log and diagnostic processing; availability of a local management mode or a European region; origin of first-, second- and third-line support; remote access policy; existence and format of the SBOM; CRA-compliant vulnerability policy; use of customer data to train models; and available certifications (ISO, ENS, national schemes). With those answers, an integrator can tell within minutes whether the product is suitable for level 1, whether it can support level 2 and what it lacks for level 3. For the vendor, filling it in once and keeping it up to date avoids answering hundreds of different questionnaires from its partners.
> For a vendor, the CADA is not a barrier if it documents early. It is a barrier if it lets its integrators discover in a tender that it cannot provide the evidence.
| Type of vendor | Impact of the CADA | Priority action |
| Non-EU infrastructure hardware | Medium: telemetry, remote management, support | Local mode and support from the EU |
| Physical infrastructure (power, cooling, racks) | Low on sovereignty, high on demand | Efficiency and European manufacturing |
| Non-EU cloud software and security | High: data, AI, support, control | EU regions, AI clauses, separation |
| European software and security | Positive | Document European added value |
| Supported open source | Positive at higher levels | Support from the EU and SBOM |

## 24. Impact on resellers and integrators
### Who they are
The value-added distributor's customers are resellers, systems integrators, managed service providers and value-added resellers of all sizes: from large consultancies such as SEIDOR, with turnover of EUR 1,174 million in 2025 [R91], or Nunsys Group, with EUR 305 million in aggregate turnover and more than 8,500 customers [R92], to regional integrators such as ACICATECH or INTEGRA and hundreds of technology SMEs. In Spain, the vast majority of partners are SMEs [R13].
### What changes for them
- Commercial: public customers will ask for the assurance level of each service, and level 1 will be the minimum condition for bidding.
- Contractual: contracts with vendors, distributors and subcontractors will have to reflect where data and telemetry are located, who provides support and from where, and what happens in the event of a third-country request.
- Strategic: each integrator will decide which part of its catalogue to take to higher levels, with which vendors and with which data centres.
### Four integrator profiles
- Large national integrator: can build its own level 2 and 3 services, operate data centres or alliances with operators and bid for large lots.
- Specialist regional integrator: competes at level 1 and, with partners, at level 2; its advantage is proximity and sector knowledge.
- Managed service provider: its service is the direct object of the CADA; it needs evidence about its own operation (operations centre, access, remote management tools).
- Product reseller: its role is to advise on and supply products whose vendors provide the evidence.
### The risk of being left out
The greatest risk for an SME integrator is that the cost of evidence and auditing for levels 2 to 4 will be disproportionate to its size. If it does not organise itself in alliances or find structured support from its distributor, it may be relegated to subcontractor for large contract winners. This risk coincides with Omdia's warning about part of today's partners disappearing by 2030 [R73].
### The opportunities
- The target of 25 % of innovative procurement going to SMEs [R1].
- The advantage of Article 17(3): an SME's level 1 declaration is valid throughout the Union, making it possible to sell to Portuguese or other Member State administrations without additional recognition.
- New services: sovereignty assessment, file preparation, compliant migrations, data space node operation, local AI.
- Critical private customers that voluntarily adopt the Article 31 assessment.
### What it means for the value chain
For integrators, the value-added distributor can make the difference between being able to offer levels 2 and 3 or not. If the distributor provides validated configurations, vendor sovereignty sheets, training, access to regional data centres with an accredited level and financing, the SME integrator can compete with the large players in CADA-adapted tenders.
### Case study: a regional integrator facing its first CADA tender
An integrator with 60 employees specialising in ERP and managed services for industry and local government receives tender specifications in 2029 requiring level 1 for the main service and level 2 for backup and the recovery environment. It can prepare the level 1 declaration on its own: it is established in Spain, its data is in a national data centre and its subcontractors are declared. For level 2 it needs an operator with an accredited level, products with European support and telemetry, and an audit. If its distributor offers it a reference configuration and an agreement with a regional operator, the incremental cost is limited to the audit of its own operation. If not, it will most likely bid in a consortium with a larger integrator or withdraw from that lot.
> The SME integrator does not have to build sovereignty alone. It has to know how to choose the products, the data centres and the distributor that allow it to demonstrate it.

## 25. The value-added distributor as sovereignty orchestrator
### From supply to orchestration
The distributor's historical value has been supply: buying in volume, financing, warehousing, delivering and providing technical support. The GTDC now describes distributors as orchestrators that provide automation, enablement, financing, marketplace operations, compliance support, data analytics and ecosystem coordination [R75][R74]. The CADA adds a new function to that list: organising the chain's sovereignty evidence.
### Five capabilities the value-added distributor already has
- A multi-vendor catalogue in infrastructure, data centre, security, cloud and AI, with hundreds of vendors in the large groups [R16].
- Technical pre-sales and professional services, which make it possible to design configurations per level.
- Training and certification through partner academies and dedicated AI units [R21].
- Financing and credit capacity for infrastructure projects.
- Presence in Spain and Portugal with a single organisation, enabling configurations valid for both national frameworks.
### Five capabilities the CADA calls for adding
- A sovereignty sheet per product family, with data provided by each vendor and translated into the language of Annexes II and III.
- Reference configurations per level (1, 2 and 3) in the highest-demand categories: data protection, virtualisation and private cloud, security, observability and local AI.
- A network of regional data centres and operators with an accredited assurance level that integrators can access (the first distributor agreements with AI-ready colocation operators point in this direction [R20]).
- A recognition support service, with audit and consulting partners.
- CRA conformity verification at product onboarding, in the role of importer or distributor [R45].
### A three-layer service model
This report's proposal (our own analysis) is to organise the value-added distributor's CADA offering in three layers:
- Information layer: sovereignty sheets, regulatory compliance matrices (CADA, CRA, NIS2, ENS, WEEE, ecodesign) and regulatory change alerts, accessible to all partners.
- Solutions layer: reference configurations per level, with pricing, financing and documentation ready for the file.
- Services layer: specialist pre-sales, training, recognition support and consortium coordination for tenders.
### Why the value-added distributor is well placed
The value-added distributor combines size, specialisation (in the large groups, the value division contributes most of the EBITDA [R18]), growth in both countries and an active policy of agreements with European and non-European vendors in security, data and infrastructure [R19]. This mix allows it to act as a neutral point between vendors of different origins, which is exactly the position needed by whoever organises the sovereignty evidence.
> The CADA turns supply chain information into a commercial asset. The distributor that standardises it and puts it at the service of its partners becomes the orchestrator of the channel's sovereignty.
| Layer | Content | Benefit for the partner |
| Information | Sovereignty sheets, regulatory matrices, alerts | Saves weeks of data gathering |
| Solutions | Configurations per level with documentation | Tender-ready offering |
| Services | Pre-sales, training, recognition, consortia | Access to levels 2–3 without an in-house structure |

## 26. Opportunities (I): sovereign infrastructure, local AI and data centre
### Workload repatriation
The CADA, the Data Act and the changes in hyperscaler programmes reinforce a trend that already existed: some workloads moving back from the public cloud to private, hybrid or regional provider environments, for reasons of cost, control or compliance. Every repatriated workload needs servers, storage, networking, virtualisation, data protection and security, which are sold through the channel.
### Private cloud and virtualisation alternatives
The closure of Broadcom's VCSP programme and licensing changes [R51] have led many customers and service providers to evaluate virtualisation alternatives and private cloud platforms. For the channel, it is a migration opportunity combining hardware, software, training and services, which can be geared from the outset towards configurations with an assurance level.
### Local AI
AI run on the customer's premises or in European data centres is the most direct answer to levels 2 and 3 for AI uses. Vendors of accelerated servers, high-performance storage and low-latency networking, together with orchestration software for open models, make up solutions that the channel can package. TD SYNNEX's experience with NVIDIA, which includes local AI Factory solutions for proofs of concept and the reservation of cloud GPU capacity for its partners, shows that the distributor can be the vehicle for this demand [R35].
### Regional data centres and edge
The wave of data centre investment (439 MW in Spain in 2025 and EUR 66.9 billion planned to 2030 [R82]) generates demand for power, cooling, racks, servers and networking, and the CADA adds a preference for European, efficient operators with European hardware. The edge (plants, hospitals, logistics centres, data space nodes) adds distributed demand that fits the channel's capillarity.
### European and efficient hardware
The criterion of hardware manufactured in the Union, both in strategic data centre projects (Art. 14) and in the European added value of public procurement (Art. 32), may favour vendors with production or assembly in the Union. The review of server ecodesign [R57] and the efficiency targets in Annex I [R1] will reward efficient equipment.
### How to capture it
- Vendors: adapt products to local operating modes and document efficiency and origin.
- Value-added distributors: reference configurations for private cloud, data protection and local AI per level; complete data centre offerings for regional operators; financing.
- Integrators: repatriation projects, migration from platforms affected by licensing changes and deployment of local AI with AI Act and CADA documentation.
### Sizing the opportunity
There are as yet no public estimates of spending on sovereign infrastructure in Spain and Portugal. A prudent approximation (our own analysis, illustrative) starts from three data points: European spending on sovereign infrastructure as a service could almost double every year between 2025 and 2027 according to Gartner [R68]; planned data centre investment in Spain reaches EUR 66.9 billion to 2030 [R82]; and partners channel two thirds of IT spending and more than 80 % of SME spending [R10][R11]. Even if only a fraction of that investment goes through value-added distributors, the potential volume is very significant for the Iberian channel. The priority is not to estimate precisely, but to be in a position to capture the share that will go through the channel.
> The CADA's first opportunity for the channel is not documentary, it is physical: every workload that returns to a controlled environment needs infrastructure, and that infrastructure is sold through the channel.
