## 7. Recognition, national authorities and procedure
### Who grants recognition and how
A provider seeking recognition of a service at a given level applies to the competent authority of the Member State where it is established (Art. 17). Each Member State designates that authority within one year (Art. 25) and gives it supervisory powers (Art. 26); the authorities cooperate through mutual assistance and cross-border cooperation (Arts. 27 and 28) [R1].
### Procedural deadlines
- The authority assesses the evidence within 60 days of admitting the application.
- The other Member States have 60 days to review it.
- Requests for information suspend the deadline, for a maximum of 30 days unless justified.
- Before a rejection, the provider has 30 days to submit observations.
- Consultations between authorities are answered within 15 days.
In practice, a level 2 to 4 recognition without incidents could be decided in around four months from admission, plus six to twelve months of evidence preparation and auditing in a mid-sized organisation (our own estimate).
### Recognised third countries
Article 18 allows the recognition of third countries whose providers could qualify for level 3 if cumulative conditions are met: adequacy under the GDPR, no controls incompatible with the Data Act, no possibility of being compelled to interrupt the service, no blocking of access to cutting-edge technology, a market open to Union providers and reciprocity in public procurement [R1]. For US, Japanese, Korean or Taiwanese vendors selling through the Iberian channel, this clause will largely determine their role at the higher levels.
### Penalties
Article 24 requires Member States to lay down effective, proportionate and dissuasive penalties, with no amounts specified in the proposal, which points to differences between countries [R1].
### Spain and Portugal
Spain will have to decide which body acts as the authority: the National Cryptologic Centre (Centro Criptológico Nacional, CCN), given its role in the National Security Framework (ENS) and product certification, or a unit of the Ministry for Digital Transformation. Portugal will have to do the same with its National Cybersecurity Centre. The choice matters: a body with certification experience will speed up recognitions; a new one may create bottlenecks.
### What it means for the channel
For integrators, recognition will be a project with an owner, a budget and a timeline. For vendors, the key is to deliver to their partners their share of the evidence in a reusable format and to communicate material changes (new support regions, changes of ownership, new components) so that the integrator can comply with Article 23. For the value-added distributor, there is room to offer recognition support: preparing the file, coordinating with auditors and maintaining the evidence, alone or with specialist partners.
> Operational risk: if 27 authorities start at the same time with few accredited auditors, the first 12 to 18 months may generate queues. Complete, early files will avoid the bottleneck.

## 8. Public-sector risk assessment and new procurement rules
### The mechanism that creates demand
If the levels are the supply side, Articles 29 to 33 create the demand. Article 29 requires Member States and Union entities to carry out, within one year and every two years thereafter, a risk assessment identifying which public activities contribute to public order and which level (2, 3 or 4) each requires, according to the sensitivity of the data, the risk of unlawful access by third countries and the risk of disruption. If the assessment requires migration, the transition may not exceed twelve months [R1].
### The procurement rule
Article 30 translates the assessment into rules:
- Bodies without public-order activities will procure services of at least level 1.
- Bodies with public-order activities will only procure level 2, 3 or 4 services, according to their assessment.
- Derogations apply where no suitable service exists, a similar tender has received no bids or compliance would be disproportionately costly [R1].
According to activeMind's analysis, public-order activities in NIS2 sectors, as well as internal security, defence, justice and law enforcement, could only use levels 2 to 4 [R3].
### Private sector
Article 31 allows private entities in the sectors listed in Annex I of NIS2 (energy, transport, banking, financial market infrastructure, health, water, digital infrastructure, ICT service management, space) to carry out voluntary assessments, and empowers the Commission to make them mandatory in highly critical sectors [R1]. For integrators serving hospitals, utilities, the food industry or logistics, this route is as relevant as the public one.
### European added value and SMEs
Article 32 requires innovative cloud and AI procurement to include non-price criteria that reward the use of technology developed in the EU, hardware manufactured in the EU and security of supply, without being decisive (recital 67 suggests up to 15 out of 120 points). Article 33 sets the target that at least 25 % of innovative cloud and AI procurement should go to innovative SMEs [R1].
### What it means for each player
For vendors, the criterion on European hardware and technology introduces points in tender specifications that do not exist today; those with manufacturing, assembly or development in the Union should document it. For integrators, each customer's risk assessment is the document to read before the tender specifications: routine administrative management will probably remain at level 1, while health, justice, security, taxation and some registries may move to levels 2 or 3. For the value-added distributor, the risk assessment makes it possible to anticipate which product families will see demand at each level and to prepare reference configurations with vendors for each case. The 25 % SME target favours the channel's base of SME integrators over the large global integrators, provided they demonstrate innovation.
### Case study: a provincial council
A provincial council (diputación) with tax management, human resources, e-government portal and municipal support services will carry out its first risk assessment around 2029 in the baseline scenario. It is reasonable to expect (our own analysis) that tax management and some registries will be classified as public-order activities, requiring level 2, while human resources, routine document management and the institutional website remain at level 1. For the channel, that council becomes two distinct markets: a level 1 market open to almost all providers, and a level 2 market where only those that have prepared the configuration and the evidence will compete. The integrator that knows the assessment before the tender will be able to propose a mixed architecture, and the distributor that has validated both configurations will be able to back several bids from different integrators.
> The Article 29 risk assessment will be the map of the public cloud market. The channel that reads it before the competition will reach the tender specifications with the configuration already validated.

## 9. Joint procurement, EuroCloud Federation and open source
### The Commission as a central purchasing body
Articles 37 to 40 allow the Commission to procure cloud services, software and AI for Union entities and Member State contracting authorities, acting as a central purchasing body following an agreement with at least two Member States. Fees are set in advance; the financial statement estimates an average fee of around 2 % of each participating authority's spending, with around EUR 6 million in annual revenue from 2029 [R1]. For small Member States, the attraction is collective purchasing power [R31].
### EuroCloud Federation
Articles 34 to 36 create a voluntary federation enabling European institutions and public bodies to share data centre capacity and cloud services. The fee would be around EUR 75,000 per member from the second year, falling to around EUR 30,000 with full participation [R1]. Part of the public infrastructure could stop being procured from the market and be obtained from other bodies instead.
### Open source
Articles 41 to 44 require the promotion of open standards and open-source components in the public cloud and AI ecosystem, the sharing of software developed for the administration in a catalogue connected to the EU's, and the creation of a network of open source programme offices (OSPOs) [R1]. Free software associations criticise the fact that the Strategy's "open source first" principle has been reduced to an obligation to promote [R32].
### What changes in the market
Centralised procurement concentrates volume in large lots that favour those with scale, and the public federation may shift the opportunity from infrastructure to the services delivered on top of it (migration, integration, applications, support). Open source redistributes value towards those who know how to deploy, integrate, maintain and secure open solutions.
### What it means for the value chain
For vendors, the large European and national framework agreements will require presence in lots with uniform conditions; working through a distributor with tendering and financing capacity makes that presence easier. For integrators, the realistic path is twofold: to be specialist partners of the winners of the large lots, and to develop a line of services based on open components that can be offered at higher levels. For the value-added distributor, the opportunity lies in aggregation: submitting multi-vendor bids for framework agreements, supporting its integrators in regional lots and expanding its catalogue of open solutions with enterprise support (for example, virtualisation platforms, software-defined storage or Kubernetes stacks with commercial support), an area where distributors are already adding vendors such as Canonical or Trilio [R19].
> Centralised procurement concentrates volume; open source redistributes it. The channel must be in both circuits: as an aggregator in the large lots and as a specialist in supported open solutions.

## 10. The AI Act and the CADA: doubly compliant AI through the channel
### Two regulations, one value chain
The AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) regulates how AI systems and models are developed, placed on the market and used according to their risk; the CADA regulates the infrastructure on which they run and the sovereignty conditions under which the public sector procures them. The former answers "what AI can do and with what safeguards"; the latter, "where it runs and under whose control" [R33].
### The AI Act after the Omnibus
The Digital Omnibus on AI reached a provisional agreement on 7 May 2026: it postpones the obligations for Annex III high-risk systems to 2 December 2027 (and those for regulated products to 2028), delays the marking of AI-generated content for systems already on the market, adds prohibitions and extends simplifications to mid-cap companies [R34]. Since 2 August 2026, the AI Office and national authorities have had supervisory powers [R33].
### Points of contact
- Training data: CADA level 2 prohibits service data from training AI of third-country entities, which adds to the AI Act's data governance requirements.
- Evidence: criterion F of Annex III (MLOps in the EU, model cards, lineage) overlaps with the AI Act's technical documentation for high-risk systems.
- Public sector: many public uses of AI are high-risk under the AI Act and, at the same time, public-order activities under the CADA.
- Consistency: the EDPS has called for explicit consistency between the two regulations [R25].
### Generative AI as a cloud service
Assistants and models consumed via API are cloud services for public procurement purposes. A body wishing to use a language model in public-order activities will need the service to be at level 2 or higher. This favours deployments of open models on European infrastructure or on the customer's own premises, and explains the growing interest in sovereign AI servers and appliances.
### The channel as the route for local AI
This point connects directly with the value-added distributor's business. AI run on premises or in a European data centre requires servers with accelerators, high-performance storage, low-latency networking, power and cooling, as well as orchestration, observability and security software. This is infrastructure sold through the channel. NVIDIA, for example, structures its European presence through specialist distributors and partners: TD SYNNEX was named NVIDIA's EMEA distributor of the year in 2026 for enabling local "AI Factory" solutions for proofs of concept [R35], and the partners of the year include specialists from southern Europe [R36]. In Iberia, distributors are already creating dedicated units to train and support partners in AI [R21].
### What it means for each player
Vendors of servers, accelerators and AI software will need to provide their partners with documentation useful for both regulations: where telemetry and updates are processed, what remote functions exist and how it is guaranteed that customer data is not used to train third-party models. Integrators that build AI into their solutions (ERP assistants, machine vision on the shop floor, document classification, biometrics) will have to classify each feature by risk under the AI Act and know where it runs under the CADA. The value-added distributor can offer "doubly compliant AI" reference configurations: validated hardware and software, run on European infrastructure, with the documentation needed for the AI Act and for the sovereignty file.
> What the AI Act asks to be documented about data and models is, to a large extent, what the CADA asks to be demonstrated about where they are processed. A single AI and data governance framework, prepared in the channel, saves work on both fronts.

## 11. The Data Act: provider switching and migrations as a business line
### The foundation of cloud competition
The Data Act (Regulation (EU) 2023/2854) has applied since 12 September 2025. Its Chapter VI regulates switching between data processing service providers: transparency, deadlines, interoperability and the gradual elimination of switching charges, which could only reflect direct costs until 12 January 2027 and are prohibited thereafter, except for early termination penalties [R37]. It also regulates access to data from connected products and safeguards against third-country governmental access to non-personal data.
### Support instruments
In November 2025 the Commission published model contractual terms and standard contractual clauses for cloud contracts, ready to be inserted [R38]. The Digital Omnibus proposes integrating other data rules into the Data Act and adjusting provider switching for custom-built services and older SME contracts [R39].
### How it fits with the CADA
- The Data Act makes it easier to leave a provider; the CADA creates reasons to do so in the public sector, with migrations of up to twelve months where the risk assessment requires it.
- Recognition of third countries under the CADA requires the absence of controls incompatible with the Data Act's provisions on access to non-personal data.
- The Data Act's interoperability reduces the technological lock-in that the CADA seeks to correct.
### The connected product also belongs to the channel
The Data Act is not just a cloud regulation. Vendors of connected products (industrial equipment, IoT devices, printers, uninterruptible power supplies, cloud-managed network equipment) must allow users to access the data they generate. Many of these products are sold through the channel, and the integrator is the one who installs and configures them. Contracts, documentation and management portals must reflect those rights.
### A wave of migrations
In practice, switching provider remains costly on platforms and applications with proprietary data and logic. If the CADA risk assessments arrive in 2029 and require migration within twelve months, a wave of simultaneous migrations may occur that strains the capacity of integrators and destination providers (our own analysis).
### What it means for the value chain
For integrators, the combination of the Data Act and the CADA opens up a service line: planning and executing compliant provider switches, with an inventory of exportable data, mapping of customisations, new contractual clauses, portability tests and documentation for the sovereignty file. For infrastructure vendors, every migration from the public cloud to private, hybrid or sovereign environments is an opportunity to sell servers, storage, and virtualisation and data protection software. For the value-added distributor, which distributes data protection, observability and storage platforms, the challenge is to package those migrations into reference offers, with financing so that the customer does not have to bear the entire investment at once.
> The Data Act is the emergency exit that makes the CADA's requirements credible. The channel that masters both regulations will sell secure migrations, not just licences or equipment.

## 12. Cybersecurity: NIS2, CSA2, EUCS and the CRA in the distribution chain
### The layer on which the CADA rests
Levels 2 to 4 require a European cybersecurity certificate at "substantial" or "high" level. It was supposed to come from the European cloud certification scheme (EUCS), blocked for years by disagreement over sovereignty requirements. The CADA takes sovereignty out of the technical scheme, but still needs a certificate; until one exists, national schemes or certificates aligned with CEN/TS 18026:2024 will apply [R1][R40].
### The Cybersecurity Act 2
On 20 January 2026 the Commission proposed replacing the Cybersecurity Act with a new regulation (CSA2) with a strengthened ENISA mandate, a more agile certification framework and a horizontal ICT supply chain security framework that allows high-risk third-country suppliers to be designated and their components to be removed from key assets, with fines of up to 7 % of global turnover [R40]. For vendors, this designation is a direct commercial risk; for the channel, it is one more reason to diversify the catalogue.
### NIS2
The NIS2 Directive includes cloud providers, data centres and managed service providers as digital infrastructure entities, subject to risk management, incident notification and supply chain security [R41]. Spain has not yet completed transposition: the Commission referred it to the Court of Justice in July 2026 and, with the dissolution of Parliament (Cortes), the law has been left for the next legislature [R42]; distributors are already seeing its pull effect on security investment and are training the channel in these frameworks [R43].
### Cyber Resilience Act: obligations for importers and distributors
The Cyber Resilience Act (CRA) imposes requirements on products with digital elements, both hardware and software. From 11 September 2026 manufacturers must report actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents; the remaining obligations, including the SBOM, apply from 11 December 2027 [R44]. The CRA also assigns obligations along the chain: the importer must check that the manufacturer has carried out the conformity assessment and that technical documentation and CE marking exist; the distributor must verify the marking and documentation before making the product available, not sell products it knows to be non-compliant and inform the manufacturer and the authorities when it detects risks [R45]. A distributor importing products from non-European vendors may take on the role of importer.
### The certification bottleneck
Dependence on a European scheme that does not exist is probably the CADA's greatest technical risk. If it is not ready, each Member State will accept its own schemes (SecNumCloud in France, C5 in Germany, ENS in Spain), with a risk of fragmentation and limited evaluator capacity in the early years.
### What it means for the value chain
Vendors must simultaneously prepare CRA conformity, the supply chain documentation required by the CADA and their exposure to possible designation as a high-risk supplier under the CSA2. A value-added distributor with a broad cybersecurity portfolio and a specialist unit can offer integrators a single control framework aligning the ENS, NIS2, the CRA and the CADA, and verify in its product onboarding process that each SKU complies with the CRA. Integrators, in turn, can turn this framework into managed security services: according to Omdia, more than 90 % of cybersecurity sales go through the channel [R12].
> CADA sovereignty rests on a certification that does not yet exist at European scale, and the CRA turns the distributor into a conformity control link. Both facts reinforce the value of the specialist distributor.
