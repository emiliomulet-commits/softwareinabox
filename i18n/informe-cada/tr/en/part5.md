## 27. Opportunities (II): services, compliance, marketplaces and financing
### Evidence and compliance services
The Annex III file requires inventories, data flow maps, subcontractor registers, software supply chain analysis and contract review. Many companies already manage part of that evidence because of the ENS, ISO 27001, the GDPR, NIS2 or DORA. Integrators with a security and compliance line can turn this experience into a recurring "sovereignty file" service, reusable in tenders and audits.
### Managed services
Managed service providers are the fastest-growing profile in the channel and the one the CADA affects most directly, because their service is a cloud service for the purposes of the regulation. With remote management tools that keep data in the Union, security operations centres on the peninsula and evidence about their own operation, they can offer level 2 services to public and critical private customers. The rise of NIS2 and DORA reinforces this line [R43].
### Marketplaces and private offers
Hyperscaler marketplaces are becoming a route to market for the channel: Microsoft already allows resale and multiparty private offers in 30 European markets [R49][R50], and partners will generate 59 % of marketplace sales by 2030 [R52]. For software vendors, publishing versions with a European region and sovereignty conditions on these marketplaces makes it easier to sell to customers with cloud spending commitments. For the value-added distributor, operating multiparty offers adds a software business line that complements infrastructure.
### Financing and consumption models
Level 2 and 3 projects require upfront investment in infrastructure, certification and services. Technology leasing and pay-per-use models (GreenLake, APEX, TruScale and distributors' own schemes) make it possible to spread that investment. The Spanish channel already has experience of financing with specialist partners, and public programmes (Kit Digital) demonstrated the channel's ability to manage grants [R66].
### Training and talent
The CADA will require profiles that are scarce today: sovereign cloud architects, compliance specialists, software supply chain auditors. Distributor and vendor partner academies can add specific learning paths on sovereignty, the AI Act and the CRA for the channel.
### Consortia and European projects
Calls under the Digital Europe Programme, Horizon Europe, Important Projects of Common European Interest and CDTI and Red.es funding lines will finance pilots of open stacks, data spaces and sovereign AI. SME integrators, with the support of a distributor that provides technology and an adviser on European funding, can take part in consortia that would otherwise be out of their reach. Software in a Box, together with Ullswater Investments, works at this intersection between European funding and technology deployment.
> The CADA's service opportunities add up: evidence, managed operation, marketplace, financing and training. The ecosystem that offers them in an integrated way will multiply its value per customer.
| Service | Who provides it | Who enables it |
| Sovereignty file | Integrator with a compliance line | Value-added distributor (sheets), vendor (data) |
| Level 2 managed services | MSP | Vendors of tools with data in the EU |
| Marketplace private offers | Value-added distributors and integrators | Software vendors |
| Financing and pay-per-use | Value-added distributors and financial partners | Vendors with consumption models |
| Sovereignty training | Distributor and vendor academies | Vendors and advisers |

## 28. Regulatory bottlenecks
### The certification that does not exist
The main regulatory bottleneck is the dependence on a European cloud cybersecurity certificate that does not exist. If the EUCS or its successor under the CSA2 is not ready, each Member State will accept its national schemes, with a risk of fragmentation between Spain (ENS), Portugal, France (SecNumCloud) and Germany (C5) [R40]. For a vendor or integrator operating in several countries, this multiplies the cost.
### Untested national authorities
Each Member State must designate a competent authority within one year. If it is created from scratch or with limited resources, the first 12 to 18 months may generate recognition queues, shifting criteria and differences of interpretation between countries (our own analysis).
### Discretion and delegated acts
Annexes II and III can be revised every 18 months by means of delegated acts. This flexibility, which several Member States want to limit [R4], introduces uncertainty: a requirement that allows level 2 today could be tightened. Contracts with vendors and customers must provide for these changes.
### Regulatory overlap
The same evidence will be required by several regulations in different formats: CADA, NIS2, CRA, DORA, AI Act, ENS, Data Act. Without a common control framework, the cost multiplies. In addition, the CRA assigns obligations to importers and distributors that the channel must build into its product onboarding processes [R45].
### Recognised third countries
The list of recognised third countries under Article 18 will decide whether many vendors in the catalogue can aspire to level 3. Until it exists, integrators will not know for certain which products they will be able to use at higher levels.
### Derogations and uneven application
Derogations on grounds of disproportionate cost or lack of supply may become the usual route for some buyers to keep buying what they always have, while others apply the regulation rigorously. This unevenness makes it difficult to plan investments.
### Mitigation from the channel
- A common control framework (CADA, NIS2, CRA, ENS) maintained by the distributor for its partners.
- Contractual clauses for adapting to changes in the annexes.
- Active monitoring of the legislative process and participation in consultations through industry associations.
> Regulatory bottlenecks do not prevent preparation, but they penalise those who prepare alone. A control framework shared across the channel reduces the cost for everyone.
| Bottleneck | Probability | Impact for the channel | Mitigation |
| Non-existent certification (EUCS) | High | Fragmentation by country | Start from the ENS and follow the CSA2 |
| Untested authorities | Medium-high | Recognition queues | Complete, early files |
| Changes to the annexes | Medium | Moving requirements | Adaptation clauses |
| Regulatory overlap | High | Multiplied cost | Common control framework |
| Third-country list | Medium | Catalogue uncertainty | Alternatives per level |

## 29. Technical and supply bottlenecks
### Memory and prices
In 2026 the memory shortage has driven early PC purchases and is putting pressure on prices in the second half of the year, according to CONTEXT [R72]. Competition for high-bandwidth memory for AI also affects servers and storage. For the channel, this means longer lead times, volatile prices and the need to plan inventory for sovereign projects.
### GPUs and AI capacity
European AI computing capacity is scarce and expensive in the short term, and the acceleration zones will not produce megawatts before 2029–2030 (our own analysis based on [R1][R70]). Distributors are seeking solutions: TD SYNNEX, for example, has reserved GPU capacity with a European cloud provider to give its partners priority access [R35]. Without access to GPUs, the level 2 and 3 local AI offering will be delayed.
### Remote management and telemetry
Many infrastructure and security products are managed today from cloud consoles with data processed outside the Union. Adapting them to local modes or European regions requires vendor development work that may take time. In the meantime, those products can only be used at level 1.
### Software supply chain
The SBOM, control of remote functions and auditing of third-country components are technically demanding, especially in firmware and in products with many dependencies. The CRA makes the SBOM mandatory from December 2027 [R44], which will help, but the depth required by levels 3 and 4 goes further.
### Simultaneous migrations
If risk assessments require migration within twelve months and many administrations do so at the same time, the capacity of integrators, vendors and destination data centres may become saturated in 2029–2030 (our own analysis).
### Energy and permits
The Spanish hourly renewables requirement for data centres of 10 MW or more, with a ramp-up to 2031 [R63][R64], may delay regional data centres that would be the natural platform for the channel's level 2 and 3 services.
### Mitigation from the channel
- Joint inventory planning with vendors for sovereign projects.
- Capacity agreements with regional data centres and European GPU providers.
- A product roadmap from each vendor for local modes and SBOMs, shared with the channel.
> The most likely technical bottleneck is not the regulation but supply: memory, GPUs, megawatts and local operating modes. The channel that plans with its vendors will have product when the tenders arrive.
| Bottleneck | Horizon | Effect | Channel response |
| Memory shortage | 2026–2027 | Prices and lead times | Inventory planning |
| GPUs and AI capacity | 2026–2029 | Delay to level 2–3 AI | Capacity agreements |
| Telemetry and remote management | 2027–2028 | Products limited to level 1 | Vendor roadmaps |
| Software supply chain (SBOM) | From 12-2027 | Documentation effort | CRA as a common basis |
| Simultaneous migrations | 2029–2030 | Capacity saturation | Early planning |

## 30. Market, margin and talent bottlenecks
### Thin margins and the cost of compliance
Channel margins are thin, especially in hardware [R8]. If preparation for the CADA is treated as a cost rather than a service, it may erode the profitability of distributors and integrators. The key is to turn evidence and support into billable services and a measurable competitive advantage.
### Concentration and consolidation
The European channel is consolidating: acquisitions between distributors, concentration of cloud business in distributors with scale because of Microsoft's thresholds [R48] and warnings that part of today's partners will not exist in their current form by 2030 [R73]. The CADA may accelerate consolidation if only the large players can afford the higher levels.
### Talent
The CADA requires scarce profiles: sovereign cloud architects, compliance specialists and auditors. The data centre sector expects more than 16,000 jobs in Spain by 2030 [R82], and the Portuguese ICT sector is growing strongly [R67]. Competition for talent will make preparation more expensive, especially for the channel's SMEs.
### Large lots versus SMEs
Centralised procurement and large framework agreements favour integrators with scale, while the 25 % SME target and automatic recognition of level 1 favour SMEs. The outcome will depend on how each Member State designs its lots and on whether SMEs organise themselves into consortia.
### Channel conflict
Hyperscalers and some vendors are increasingly selling directly or through marketplaces. Omdia estimates that vendors' direct spending will grow almost three times faster than spending through partners in 2026, largely due to infrastructure sales to hyperscalers [R93]. By rewarding control and local support, the CADA may restore weight to the channel in the sovereign segment, but the risk of disintermediation persists.
### Mitigation from the channel
- Billable sovereignty services, with clear pricing and packages per level.
- Shared training across the ecosystem (distributor and vendor academies).
- Consortia of SME integrators for large lots, coordinated by the distributor.
- Channel presence on marketplaces through multiparty private offers.
> The CADA's market risk is that sovereignty becomes a privilege of the large players. The value-added distributor is the player best placed to prevent this, by sharing capabilities with the channel's SMEs.
| Risk | Affected | Mitigation |
| Margin erosion | Distributors and integrators | Billable sovereignty services |
| Accelerated consolidation | SME integrators | Consortia and distributor support |
| Talent shortage | The whole channel | Shared training |
| Large lots | SMEs | Alliances and specialist subcontracting |
| Disintermediation | Distributors and resellers | Marketplace and local value |

## 31. Scenarios 2027–2030 and joint roadmap
### Three scenarios
- Soft scenario (medium probability, our own analysis): the CADA is approved with watered-down requirements and broad derogations; level 1 dominates; levels 2 to 4 are a niche. The channel gains mainly from the data centre wave and level 1 documentation.
- Baseline scenario (highest probability): agreement in the fourth quarter of 2027, application in 2029; level 1 as the general rule and levels 2 and 3 in health, justice, taxation, security and critical infrastructure, with voluntary adoption by part of the critical private sector. The channel that has configurations per level and evidence ready captures the first wave of tenders in 2029–2030.
- Strict scenario (low-to-medium probability): high levels for broad public categories and mandatory assessments in highly critical private sectors. Vendors with no European alternative lose public market share and demand for sovereign infrastructure accelerates.
### Roadmap for the value-added distributor
- 2027: create the sovereignty sheet and request it from the main vendors; classify the catalogue by potential level; launch training on the CADA, CRA and AI Act; close agreements with regional data centres.
- 2028: publish level 1, 2 and 3 reference configurations; support the first integrators through recognition; prepare bids for framework agreements.
- 2029–2030: operate the evidence service at scale; coordinate consortia; expand to Portugal and other markets.
### Roadmap for vendors
- 2027: document telemetry, support, control and software supply chain; prepare CRA conformity.
- 2028: offer local management modes or European regions; support from the Union; SBOM; clauses on not training with customer data.
- 2029–2030: add sovereign deployment competencies to their partner programmes.
### Roadmap for integrators
- 2027: service inventory, dependency map and level 1 self-assessment; decide which services to take to higher levels.
- 2028: prepare the file with the distributor's help; choose data centres; train teams; seek alliances.
- 2029–2030: bid in the first tenders requiring specific levels; offer evidence and migration services to their customers.
> The roadmap works in all three scenarios because it rests on the stable elements of the proposal: levels, evidence, risk assessment and European added value.
| Year | Value-added distributor | Vendors | Integrators |
| 2027 | Sovereignty sheet, catalogue classification, training | Document control, support and telemetry | Inventory and level 1 self-assessment |
| 2028 | Configurations per level, recognition support | Local modes, EU support, SBOM | File, data centres, alliances |
| 2029 | Evidence service at scale, consortia | Sovereign competencies in programmes | First CADA tenders |
| 2030 | Extension to Portugal and other markets | Consolidated tiered catalogue | Migration and evidence services |

## 32. Conclusions and recommendations
### Conclusions
- The CADA organises public procurement of cloud and AI by sovereignty levels and creates a seal that critical private customers will also demand [R1][R3].
- The level is granted to the service, but the evidence is spread across the whole chain: vendor, distributor and integrator. None of them can demonstrate it alone.
- The Iberian channel comes to the CADA on a growth path, with Spain and Portugal above the European average and the value segment growing faster than the whole [R7][R6][R71].
- The first opportunity is physical: data centres, workload repatriation, private cloud and local AI. The second is in services: evidence, managed operation, marketplace, financing and training.
- The most likely bottlenecks are the non-existent certification, untested authorities, the supply of memory and GPUs, energy for new data centres and talent.
- The value-added distributor is well placed to be the orchestrator of the Iberian channel's sovereignty: it has the catalogue, specialisation, presence in both countries, training and financing.
### Recommendations for value-added distributors
- Create a standard sovereignty sheet per product family in 2027 and make it an onboarding requirement for new vendors.
- Launch reference configurations per level in data protection, private cloud, security, observability and local AI.
- Integrate the CADA, CRA, NIS2 and ENS into a single control framework for their partners.
- Expand the network of regional data centres and operators with an accredited level.
- Add sovereignty to their partner academies.
### Recommendations for vendors
- Document telemetry, support, control and software supply chain now, and share it with the channel.
- Offer local management modes or European regions and support from the Union.
- Prepare CRA conformity and the SBOM.
- Design sovereign deployment competencies in their partner programmes.
### Recommendations for integrators
- Inventory their services and self-assess against level 1 in 2027.
- Choose one or two services to take to level 2 or 3 with the distributor's support.
- Turn their compliance experience into a sovereignty file service.
- Explore consortia for large lots and European projects.
> The CADA will reward chains, not links. Vendors, distributor and integrators that organise themselves together in 2027 and 2028 will be in the front row when public administrations start buying by level.
| Player | First action (2027) | Expected outcome (2029) |
| Value-added distributor | Sovereignty sheet and tiered catalogue | Orchestrator of the channel's evidence |
| Vendors | Document control, support and telemetry | Products eligible for levels 2–3 |
| Integrators | Inventory and level 1 self-assessment | Tiered offering in CADA tenders |
