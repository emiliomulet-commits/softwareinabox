Why this section is different

Sovereignty is not proven by a single player. It is proven by a chain.

Cloud and AI Development Act (CADA)

The first EU regulation to turn digital sovereignty into verifiable requirements for buying cloud and AI. It organises the public market into four assurance levels, requires public administrations to assess risks and aims to triple Europe's data-centre capacity in five to seven years.

The level is granted to the service, but it is earned along the chain. A manufacturer that does not provide its share of the evidence leaves its integrators out of levels 2 to 4.

The three pillars

1R&D&I

Cloud and AI leadership initiatives, AI Centres built on the digital innovation hubs, national cloud and AI strategies within one year, and frontier AI projects.

Arts. 3–9 · Title II

2Capacity

Triple data-centre capacity in 5–7 years. Each Member State proposes at least one acceleration zone within 6 months; strategic projects with fast-track permits and a single point of contact.

Arts. 10–15 · Title III

3Autonomy

A sovereignty framework with four assurance levels, public-sector risk assessment, European added-value criteria, central purchasing, the EuroCloud Federation and promotion of open source.

Arts. 16–44 · Title IV

The four assurance levels (Annex II)

Voluntary for the provider, but they decide what the administration can buy for each activity. The critical jump is between level 1 and level 2.

1
Self-assessment
  • Provider established in the EU
  • Infrastructure, data, metadata and telemetry in the EU
  • Support outside the EU without compromising autonomy
  • State-of-the-art cybersecurity
  • An SME's declaration is valid across the EU (art. 17(3))
2
Audit · "substantial"
  • Provider, subcontractors, infrastructure and staff in the EU
  • Support initiated and delivered only from the EU
  • Ban on using the data to train third-country AI
  • SBOM, control of remote functions, migration plan
  • Separation from the non-EU parent company
3
Audit · "substantial"
  • Staff with EU citizenship and security clearance where applicable
  • Provider not controlled by a third country
  • Unless a derogation linked to a recognised third country applies (art. 18)
4
Audit · "high"
  • No third-country control
  • Sensitive data always in the EU
  • Effective control of the entire software supply chain

How demand is created: risk assessment and procurement rules

Art. 29 · Risk assessment

Member States and EU bodies assess, within one year and every two years thereafter, which public activities contribute to public order and which level (2, 3 or 4) each one requires. If migration is needed, the transition may not exceed 12 months.

Art. 30 · Procurement rule

No public-order activities: at least level 1. With public-order activities: levels 2, 3 or 4. Derogations apply if no suitable service exists, the tender receives no bids or compliance is disproportionately costly.

Art. 31 · Critical private sector

Entities in the sectors of Annex I of NIS2 (energy, transport, banking, health, water, digital infrastructure…) may assess themselves voluntarily; the Commission may make it mandatory in highly critical sectors.

Arts. 32–33 · European value and SMEs

Criteria for technology developed and hardware manufactured in the EU (up to 15 of 120 points according to recital 67) and a target that 25% of innovative cloud and AI procurement goes to SMEs.

What your integrators will ask of you

  • Where telemetry, logs and diagnostics are processed, and whether they can stay in the EU or be switched off.
  • Local management mode or management from a European region, with no mandatory console outside the EU.
  • 2nd- and 3rd-level support from the EU, with controlled and logged remote access.
  • SBOM and a vulnerability policy in line with the CRA.
  • A clause stating that customer data does not train AI models.
  • Ownership structure and policy on requests from third-country authorities.

From supplying to orchestrating

For thousands of SKUs, the wholesaler already maintains producer registration (WEEE), efficiency (ecodesign), marking and warranties. The CADA adds one more column to each SKU's sheet: sovereignty. And the CRA turns it into a conformity-control link as importer or distributor.

A three-layer service model

InformationSovereignty sheets, regulatory matrices (CADA, CRA, NIS2, ENS, WEEE, ecodesign) and change alerts
SolutionsReference configurations per level with price, financing and documentation for the file
ServicesSpecialised presales, training, recognition support and tender consortia

Wholesaler checklist

  • Is there a sovereignty sheet per product family? (B, C, H, I)
  • Are CRA conformity and marking checked when a product is listed?
  • Are first-level support and spare-parts logistics delivered from the EU? (B, H)
  • Are there reference configurations per level? (Annex II)
  • Are manufacturers' material changes communicated to partners? (art. 23)
The CADA turns supply-chain information into a commercial asset. The wholesaler that standardises it becomes the orchestrator of the channel's sovereignty.

Integrator checklist

  • Service-providing entity established in the EU (A)
  • Infrastructure, backups and recovery in the EU (B)
  • Data, metadata and telemetry in the EU (C)
  • Declared subcontractors (H)
  • State-of-the-art cybersecurity: ENS, ISO/IEC 27001 (E)
  • File owner and material-change procedure (art. 23)
  • If you are an SME, automatic recognition of level 1 (art. 17(3))
An SME integrator does not have to build sovereignty alone. It has to know how to choose the products, data centres and wholesaler that allow it to prove it.

If you are in the public sector

  • First risk assessment (art. 29) in the year following application; then every two years.
  • Ordinary administration at level 1; health, justice, taxation and security probably at levels 2–3.
  • Required migrations within 12 months at most: plan them with the Data Act.
  • European added-value criteria and 25% of innovative procurement for SMEs.

If you are an industry or critical service (NIS2)

  • Voluntary assessment today (art. 31); possible sector obligation tomorrow.
  • The assurance level will become a label you can require from your cloud, AI and managed-service providers.
  • Combine it with NIS2 (supply chain) and DORA (finance) in a single supplier questionnaire.

What to ask your supplier from today

  • Target CADA level for each service and a timeline to reach it.
  • A map of where data, backups, logs and telemetry are.
  • Where support is provided from and who can access your systems.
  • A contractual guarantee that your data does not train AI.
  • An exit and portability plan in line with the Data Act (no charges from 12-1-2027).
  • AI Act classification of each AI feature you are going to deploy.
For the end customer, the CADA is a negotiating lever: it turns "trust us" into "prove it with auditable evidence".

Bottlenecks and market effects

What could slow things down, and how to get ahead of it

The certification that does not exist

Levels 2–4 require a "substantial" or "high" European certificate, but the EUCS is still blocked. Meanwhile: ENS in Spain, SecNumCloud in France, C5 in Germany. Risk of fragmentation.

Untested authorities

Twenty-seven authorities starting at once with few accredited auditors: possible queues in the first 12–18 months.

Annexes reviewable every 18 months

Delegated acts can tighten requirements. Contracts should include adaptation clauses.

Regulatory overlap

The same evidence is requested by the CADA, NIS2, CRA, DORA, AI Act, ENS and Data Act in different formats. Without a common control framework, costs multiply.

Recognised third countries

The art. 18 list will decide whether many US, Japanese, Korean or Taiwanese manufacturers can aim for level 3.

Derogations and uneven enforcement

"Disproportionate cost" may become the route some buyers use to keep buying the same as always.

Memory and prices

Memory shortages in 2026 and competition for high-bandwidth memory for AI: long lead times and volatile prices for servers and storage.

GPUs and AI capacity

European compute is scarce and expensive in the short term; acceleration zones will not deliver megawatts before 2029–2030.

Remote management and telemetry

Products managed from consoles outside the EU will only qualify for level 1 until the manufacturer offers a local mode.

Software supply chain

SBOM mandatory under the CRA from 11-12-2027; levels 3–4 require going further: firmware, remote functions and third-country components.

Simultaneous migrations

If many administrations have to migrate within 12 months at the same time, integrators and data centres could become saturated in 2029–2030.

Energy and permits

In its revised version, the Spanish draft royal decree requires data centres of 10 MW or more to cover 80% of their consumption with renewables generated in the same hour, with transition periods until 2031.

Thin margins

With wholesaler margins of 3–8% on hardware, compliance must become a billable service, not a cost.

Channel consolidation

Up to 30% of today's partners may not exist in their current form by 2030. The CADA could accelerate this if only the big players reach the higher levels.

Talent

Sovereign-cloud architects, compliance specialists and software-supply-chain auditors: scarce and expensive profiles.

Large lots versus SMEs

Centralised procurement favours scale; the 25% for SMEs and automatic level 1 favour them. Consortia are the key.

Disintermediation

Hyperscalers and manufacturers increasingly sell direct or through marketplaces. By valuing local support, the CADA could give weight back to the channel.

Vendor programmes in flux

Microsoft CSP thresholds, Broadcom's VCSP closure, Cisco 360, HPE Partner Ready Vantage: the partner map is being redrawn.

Scenarios 2027–2030

Three scenarios and one roadmap that works in all three

Soft · medium probability

Lower requirements and broad derogations; level 1 dominates and levels 2–4 are a niche. The channel gains mainly from the data-centre wave.

Base · highest probability

Agreement in Q4 2027 and application in 2029. Level 1 across the board; levels 2–3 in health, justice, taxation, security and critical infrastructure. First wave of tenders in 2029–2030.

Strict · low-to-medium probability

High levels for broad public categories and mandatory assessments in critical private sectors. Demand for sovereign infrastructure accelerates.

Qualitative probabilities: Software in a Box's own analysis, not official forecasts.
